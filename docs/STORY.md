# Blue Hour: the story

One evening at the Shibuya Scramble, Saturday 10 October 2026, from the blue hour into the night. One camera with
seven frames left in it. One question that turns over with every frame: **why did he not cross the street?**

This is the story bible. It is one episode, one case played in one go, in seven frames. It holds the premise and its
rules; what was learned from the works it stands on; the real Shibuya it is made of; the people, each with their life
before, during and after; the truth of what happened, minute by minute; why each of them kept silent; the case frame
by frame; every clue and where it is planted; and how the game tells it. `cities/shibuya/city.json` `game.story` holds
the episode as data (`web/game/story.js` plays it; `tools/story_play.js` plays it through).

The people and the crime are invented. The place, the dates, the hours and the city's history are real, and the story
is built so that the real things are the clues.

## 1. The premise in one breath

On 10 October 2026, at 17:14, as the blue hour begins, the crossing fills and the world stops. Only you still move, and
a young woman at the kerb dressed for 1964. She has waited on that corner at that hour for sixty-two years for a man who
never came. She gives you his camera. There are seven frames left on a roll he began in 1964, and the twenty-nine frames
before them were never developed.

Each photograph you take in the stopped world, from the right place, brings up in the finder a thing of that day that
stood there (a board, a boy's empty stool, a police box's lamp, a dome), laid over tonight like a second exposure. Seven
frames, seven things out of the past, one evening.

He came. He stood across the road from her, between two policemen, and did not cross. The story is finding out why, and
then doing something about it with the last frame.

## 2. The rules

A mystery is only fair if its impossible thing has rules, said early and never broken. These are all of them; the
right-hand column is where the player meets each one.

| # | Rule | Where it is shown |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **An afterimage** is what a person leaves at a place, at the hour they came nearest to doing a thing they meant to do there, and did not. Everyone leaves them, the living too. Most are gone by morning, because tomorrow the thing can still be done. One stays when it never can: the train has gone, or the person is dead. It is not a ghost | Shun, frame 2 (his grandfather's saying); the old woman, frame 7 (the girl is hers, and she is alive) |
| 2 | **An afterimage keeps to its place**, as it was at that hour. It knows what its person knew then, and what has passed within a few steps of it since; so it can count the years. Where its place has been pulled down, it stands where the place can still be seen | The girl, frame 1 (*I cannot see past this kerb, and I cannot see a photograph*); Kanbara over the roof that is gone, frame 4 (*Is it there? The dome?*); Tatsuo is told what the roof saw, frame 5; Akira, frame 6 (*I can never see past the road*) |
| 3 | **The Stop.** In it the world has no time: people, traffic, trains, rain and screens hold. Hachi, the dog who leads you, is the one figure in the Stop that these rules do not account for, and nobody explains
him: the dog of the bronze, who came to the station every evening for nearly ten years for a man who did not come back.
He moves in the Stop, comes to you the first time it comes, and goes home when the case is closed. A story about waiting
has the most famous waiter in Japan at its door; that is enough. He is the guide, never a witness: he says nothing and
knows nothing the player needs.

## 3. What the best of them do, and what is taken from each

The brief was a plot that pulls like a short serial drama and satisfies like a detective novel. These are the works
studied, what makes each one work, and what Blue Hour adopts. Nothing of their look, characters or text is used.

| Work | What makes it work | What Blue Hour takes |
|---|---|---|
| *Return of the Obra Dinn* (2018) | Deduction from frozen moments; the game never tells you that you are right until several answers agree | The Stop is a frozen moment you read for evidence. A photograph is how you commit to an answer, and the double exposure is the confirmation |
| *Her Story* (2015), *Immortality* (2022) | The player assembles the truth out of order; testimony that is sincere and still wrong | Each afterimage tells only what they knew at their hour. Nobody lies outright except one; the truth is in where the accounts do not fit |
| *Outer Wilds* (2019) | A short fixed span; the only thing you carry forward is what you know | One evening, forty minutes. Nothing is levelled up; what you keep is the roll and the file |
| *Ace Attorney* (2001–) | One contradiction per testimony, broken by presenting one piece of evidence; a reversal every case | Tatsuo is cross-examined: the player picks, from his own statements, the one the roll will not let stand and the print that breaks it, and neither is named for them; Mr Tanaka trusts you only when shown his pitch. Every frame ends turned over |
| *Ghost Trick* (2010) | A last reveal about who someone is that makes every earlier scene mean something else, with every clue shown in plain sight | Sumiko is alive: revealed in frame 7, planted in the first Stop (*You came late*, in an old woman's voice) |
| *Disco Elysium* (2019) | The detective's own reason for being there is part of the case | Who hired you, why at 17:14, and why you, of all people, take jobs on the tenth of October |
| *Firewatch* (2016) | A voice on the radio carries a life the player has walked away from | Kagami's sister: the office's word in the first minute, a message in the middle, a call on the last card |
| *428: Shibuya Scramble* (2008) | Shibuya itself as the board; hour stamps; "to be continued" as a weapon | Real hours on every print, the district as the map of the case, every frame ending on a question |
| *Life is Strange* (2015) | A photograph as the hinge of time; an ending that is one act, not a boss | The last frame is one photograph, and taking it is the ending |
| *Paranormasight* (2023), *AI: The Somnium Files* (2019) | Local history turned into the engine of the mystery | 1964 Shibuya is not set dressing: each real fact is a clue (section 4) |
| K. Higashino, *The Devotion of Suspect X* (2005) | A howdunit whose last turn is not who did it but what someone gave up, and for whom | Two people each took the other's guilt on themselves and kept silent for sixty years, each for the same reason |
| A. Christie; the Knox and Van Dine rules | Fair play: every clue before the reader, the answer the least expected of the people already met | Section 9: every answer is shown at least a frame before it is needed |
| Short serial dramas (vertical dramas, 2022–) | A hook in the first thirty seconds, one reversal per episode, an ending on the worst possible second, a secret identity paid off late, feeling stated plainly | The shape of every frame: a hook, one task, one reversal, a question. No frame over six minutes |
| M. Shinkai, *Your Name* (2016) | The hour of twilight as the only time two worlds touch | The blue hour as the door, and rain kept for the last frame |

Three rules come out of this and govern every frame:

- **One question per frame, answered, and a worse one asked in the same breath.**
- **The player proves it; nobody explains it.** A witness says what they saw and did, never what it means. A reveal
  arrives as a print in the player's hand before any character says it aloud.
- **The city is the witness.** The sun's hour, a sight line, a building that was not there yet or is not there now: the
  evidence is geometry the map really has, so a player who looks can get ahead of the story.

## 5. The people

Who each of them is, what they want and fear, what they hide, what they do on the day and in the sixty-two years after,
where the player meets them, and how they speak. Ages are as of 10 October 1964 and of tonight.

### Hayase Sumiko 早瀬澄子 (22; 84 tonight)

- **Then.** Born 1942. Her father died in the war when she was three; her mother is often ill; her brother Tatsuo, nine
  years older, keeps the house. A lift girl at the Toyoko store over the station since she was sixteen: white gloves, a
  little hat, the floors called in a bright voice, and the lift girls' rule, *you take people up, and you do not ask
  them why*. Quick, proud, practical, a little sharp; she decides things and does not explain them.
- **Want.** To leave on the night train with Akira and be nobody's arrangement. **Fear.** Being given to Kanbara, to
  whom her brother owes more than money.
- **Secret.** She was on the planetarium's roof at three. She tore free of Kanbara and shoved him, and ran. She left
  him on his feet, and her glove in his hand.
- **Belief, 1964 to 1989.** That Akira killed Kanbara, for her. **Silence.** If she had said she was on that roof, it
  would have been murder for a woman; without her, it was a quarrel over a roll of film. So she said nothing, and never
  went to him: they would have asked her why. The same reason, word for word, that kept him on his kerb.
- **After.** The lift until the store's lifts went automatic, then its information desk until she retired. Asked to
  marry, she said she was waiting for someone, and they stopped asking. In 1989 her brother told her the truth on his
  deathbed. She looked for a Mizuno; there was none to find. She never opened the camera (his rule: *light kills what is
  inside*) and never had it developed: first for fear of what Kanbara had wanted on it, then for fear there would be
  nothing left, and always because as long as it was shut, there was one picture of his she had not seen. Every tenth of
  October at sunset she stands at her kerb for an hour with the camera in her bag, looking east, the way he would come
  from the dome. Never once across the road.
- **In the case.** The girl at the kerb (frames 1 and 7); the old woman in the held crowd, whose voice says *You came
  late* in the first Stop and who turns in the sixth; the one who asks the three questions (frame 7); the one who
  crosses. **Voice.** At 22, quick and bright, with an edge (*I was not waiting for you*). At 84, dry and exact, a
  supervisor's: *let me see what you know*. She says what she feels once, at the end, plainly.

### Mizuno Akira 水野晶 (26; died in March 2026, aged 87)

- **Then.** Born 1938. A lecturer at the Gotoh Planetarium since 1960. Every clear night he photographs the sky from the
  dome's roof, month by month, to record the stars the city's lights are putting out; it is the subject of his lectures.
  Careful (his name is scratched on the base plate where his hand would cover it), patient, a man of small rules (*never
  open a camera*), and quietly romantic: he chose the night train for its name. A planetarium in Osaka has a post for
  him from November.
- **Want.** To leave with her on the 10th. **Fear.** Harming her. He would rather be the worst thing that happened to
  her than the cause of anything worse.
- **On the day.** In the darkroom under the dome all afternoon, printing slides for the seven o'clock lecture: a
  darkroom has no window, and nobody opens its door. When the jets come over he runs up the iron stair to photograph the
  rings (the twenty-eighth frame of his roll), passing a man in work clothes coming down whom he does not look at. Below
  the parapet he finds Kanbara dead, a white glove in his fist with a darn in the thumb in blue thread: he had watched
  her sew it on the tram. He does not stop to ask her: if he asked, he would have to hear it. In the dark he pencils one
  word on the back of her ticket, *wait*, and puts it under the base plate. He walks into the police box nearest her
  corner, so that he can ask for one minute there at sunset, says he pushed Kanbara, and says the glove is his. In his
  minute he photographs her corner from the station kerb (the twenty-ninth frame), and sends the camera across with a
  boy and one sentence: *tell her to open it*.
- **After.** Twelve years for murder, on his confession. Out in 1976 under his mother's name, Hoshino 星野. He finds the
  boy and asks whether she heard it; told yes, he believes she opened the camera, read *wait*, and chose not to. He
  will not ask her twice. A room in Sangenjaya, fifteen minutes down the old tram line; thirty years as the darkroom
  printer of a small office that photographs streets for the ward, where among the old plans he finds the one of her
  corner and pencils the same word on it. Every tenth of October at sunset, one minute at the station kerb, looking at
  her corner, not at the people on it; then home. In October 2025, ill, he means to cross at last; the lights go green,
  and he steps back. He dies in March. His will pays his old office for one photograph of her corner at 17:14 on the
  tenth, before the kerb laid in 1964 is taken up on Monday: a man who spent his life photographing what the city was
  losing, recording one more thing before it went. He never knew who came down the stair.
- **In the case.** The man not in any photograph, because he took them all; the client (*the estate of A. Hoshino*); the
  afterimage of 2025 on his kerb (frame 6) and in the last frame. **Voice.** Quiet and exact; he says what he did, never
  what he felt, except once: *I did not mean for the rest of hers*.

### Hayase Tatsuo 早瀬辰雄 (31; died in 1989, aged 56)

- **Then.** Born 1933. Foreman on the Shibuya River works, half his life on the works. The family's wage since the war;
  proud of his men, hot-tempered, a drinker, and in Kanbara's pocket.
- **Secret.** On the night of 17 September he ran a pour at night to make the Games' date, and the river came down on a
  boy of his gang, Kudo Masao, who drowned in the culvert. He took Kanbara's money to say the boy fell in drunk on his
  night off. On the 10th, having watched the building's door from the street since half past two (he had heard the plan
  at his own table the night before), he went up the stair after Kanbara, found him at the parapet with his back turned, watching the
  rings, a lift girl's white glove in his fist, and pushed him without a word. Coming down, he passed the lecturer going
  up.
- **On the day, after.** At half past five he set out from the works to the police box to tell them. At the foot of the
  store by Miyamasu Bridge he heard that the lecturer had given himself up, and turned round. That is his afterimage's
  hour, and from it he lies.
- **Why silent.** It would have hanged him; it kept his sister's name out of it; and Kanbara's death freed them both. He
  told Sumiko on his deathbed in 1989, about the roof. He never knew she had the camera.
- **In the case.** The story's one liar (frame 5): an alibi the roof can see through. Broken, he says only what he did
  and saw, and he says the drowned boy's name for the first time. **Voice.** Short, blunt, a working man's pride: *nobody
  will thank us for it*.

### Kanbara Soichi 神原宗一 (38; died at about 15:11 on 10 October 1964)

- **Then.** Section chief for the contractor on the river works. Ambitious and frightened in equal parts; a bully who
  calls it management; the Games' date above everything. He wrote the drowned boy down as off duty and paid the foreman.
  The Hayase family owes him, and he means to marry Sumiko, who will not look at him.
- **Why the roll.** He had watched the lecturer climb the dome's roof at night with his camera, and his works are at the
  bottom of every picture. A sky photograph keeps its hour: the stars say when. On a night the book says the works were
  dark, his lamps were lit. The lecturer would not sell the roll. On the 9th, drunk at the Hayase table, he told Tatsuo
  he would take it while the city watched the ceremony, and that if September came out, the book would say the foreman
  ran the pour. Sumiko heard.
- **On the day.** Up at a quarter to three; the roof empty, the cut below empty (he had sent the men home). The girl came
  instead; he took her wrist and asked where the lecturer kept his film; she tore loose and ran. He stood at the parapet
  with her glove, watching the jets draw the rings, and never turned round.
- **In the case.** The victim who never saw his killer, and so the one afterimage who wants the truth as much as the
  player does (frame 4). He stands on SHIBUYA SKY's roof, over the place his roof was pulled down (rule 2). **Voice.**
  Orders: *Write that down.*

### Mr Tanaka 田中さん (14; 76 tonight)

- **Then.** A shoeshine man's son who minded his father's pitch at the station kerb on Saturday afternoons, the box his
  father made in front of him. Called over by a man standing between a detective and a constable, given a hundred yen,
  the man's camera and one sentence for the girl by the pillar box: *tell her to open it*. Half way across, a policeman
  on her side turned to look at him: a shoeshine boy running with a camera like that. He pushed it into her hands and
  ran. He never said it, and never saw her face.
- **After.** A shoe repairer in Sangenjaya's lanes. In 1977 a man with a new name came looking for the boy and asked
  whether she had heard it. He said yes. The lie became the ground of the man's peace, and Tanaka could not take it away
  and give nothing back: he did not know where she was, nor which of the old women at that corner she might be. Forty
  years at the same bath-house. Every tenth of October he stood on the stone blossom in the square, where the man had
  stood with the police in 1964, behind his friend at the kerb; his friend never once turned round. He witnessed the
  will.
- **In the case.** The old man on the blossom all evening, the one Shun speaks of (frame 2), who has been watching the
  camera (frame 5), who asks to be shown his pitch and then tells the rest (frame 6). **Voice.** Gentle, ashamed, a
  craftsman's exactness: *the box my father made*. *Sixty years I have had it in my mouth.*

### The detective (about 45 in 1964)

Twenty years on the force. Knows a lie when it stands in front of him, and knows when a confession is too fast. On the
day of the Games, with a confession, a store that says every girl was at her post and a superior who wants it closed,
he closed it within the hour, let a man give his own camera to a boy, and lost the boy in the crowd. His afterimage is
of that minute: the thing he meant to do and did not was follow the glove. **In the case:** frame 3, the hub of
testimony; the false solution (*it was her*) is offered to him. **Voice:** dry, fair, ironic: *In twenty years nobody has
confessed to me that politely.*

### Kagami 鏡 (about 30), the player

- **Who.** A photographer who photographs streets for a living and takes a small office's jobs: a kerb as it stands
  before works. Played as a woman or a man; camel over black, a working camera on the chest (section 10, *Who you are, to
  look at*). The name is a mirror, and the office uses no other.
- **Secret.** Three years ago today their mother died while Kagami was out on a job, and Kagami came too late. At the
  funeral their sister said *don't bother coming any more*. Kagami took it at its word. Every tenth of October since,
  Kagami has taken a job, and has the office tell the sister what it always tells her: *out on a job*. The man who would
  not cross, sixty years on, in someone else.
- **Arc.** Late (17:15), as at the funeral, and greeted with it: *You came late*. The first person in sixty-two years to
  turn and look at the girl. Carries his camera across the road, which he never could. Hears the sister's message in the
  middle of the evening (*I didn't mean for the rest of our lives*), and then Akira's own (*I did not mean for the rest
  of hers*). On the last card, standing at a kerb, rings the number.

### Kagami's sister

Rings the office three times this year and once today; leaves a message as Kagami comes down from the roof (frame 5):
she is at their mother's, three years today, has lit the incense for both of them, and did not mean it for the rest of
their lives. *Ring me, or don't. I'm here.* The last card answers her.

### Kudo Masao 工藤正男 (19; drowned on the night of 17 September 1964)

A seasonal hand from Aomori who came down for the Games' works. Written in the book as a drunk who fell in the river on
his night off. Never seen; named once, by Tatsuo, for the first time since. The crime at the root of the case, and the
reason for every other: on Akira's roll, if sixty-two years have left anything, is a night in September with the works'
lamps lit under the stars.

### The living who help

- **Shun 駿**, at the vintage shop in Center Gai (古着屋), where old cameras sit among the clothes (frame 2). Reads the
  camera; his grandfather had the shop in '64
  and said the things that are the story's rules (*people leave themselves behind…*; *a roll wakes one frame at a time,
  if you stand where the man stood*). Thought he was drunk.
- **Yuna 優奈**, at SHIBUYA SKY's lift (frame 4). Her grandmother ran the lift beside Sumiko's: saw the Hayase girl come
  back from the planetarium with one glove, heard the floor manager tell the police every girl was at her post, said
  nothing, and was sorry for it all her life. *You take people up, and you do not ask them why.*
- **The office 事務所**, a voice on the phone (the first minute; the helicopter). Sends Kagami, gives the job and its odd
  word, and keeps telling the sister *out on a job*. The client is *an estate: a Mr Hoshino, who printed for us before my
  time*.

## 8. The case, frame by frame

**The shape.** A detective story is cut by its questions, not by a quota: the reader stays for as long as each answer
opens a worse question, and is let go once, at the end. So the case is **one episode, played in one go**: one evening
that runs in real time from 17:14 into the night, seven frames, about thirty-five to forty-five minutes, and one card at
the end with the whole roll on it. The seven frames are the clock of the case: the counter goes down, and there is no
eighth.

It has the movements a classical mystery has:

- **A false solution at the midpoint** (frame 3): the glove points at Sumiko herself.
- **A liar broken by evidence** (frame 5): Tatsuo cross-examined, his own statements against the roll.
- **A witness who trusts only what he is shown** (frame 6): Mr Tanaka and his pitch.
- **The challenge to the reader** (frame 7): the living Sumiko asks three questions, each answered with a name (one of
  the case's people, from all of them) and then the print (or the pair of prints) that proves it, with the roll and the
  file open to the player from inside the question. A right name on the wrong print is not an answer. It is never lost:
  a wrong answer is answered and written against the player in the file, and the third on one question she answers
  herself (section 12, "The first play, from what players did"). The second is the title's own question, and its answer is the one inference nobody
  says aloud: whom Akira was keeping out of it. The player says it to her face.
- **The last reveal** (frame 7, and the card): she is alive; the camera was hers all along; the word.

## 10. How it is told

### Who you are, to look at

Kagami photographs streets for a living and takes an office's jobs (a kerb as it stands

For most of the evening the player sees Kagami's back, at the chase camera's distance, in a crowd of thousands at

colours and what it carries before its face, and to look like Tokyo in October:

- **Camel over black.** The colours of a trench coat and a black turtleneck, the detective's and the artist's, in

### The title, as a game opens (November 2026)

The owner asked for the opening to be rebuilt as a AAA game's is, to stun at a glance, with who you play on a screen of

- **What a title is for, in order.** To make the promise before a word is read: this place, this hour, this mood (a
  crossing at dusk and the people in it). To get the one press a browser waits for before it plays a sound, so the
  score can come in. And to ask what has to be asked, each thing at the moment it has to be, not before.
- **Attention is one thing at a time.** Everything on a screen competes with everything else on it, so a screen asks
  one question: the press, *are you here?*; the menu, *which way in?*; who you play, *who are you?*; the walk's step,
  *when?* The old card asked three of them at once.
- **The image makes the promise, not the type.** The press is the living street at the blue hour with only the name
  over it: the frame letterboxed, which is how a film says *a story begins*; the name arriving rather than being there
  (its letters close in out of a blur); the line under it; the prompt last, breathing, so the eye has time for the
  picture before it is asked for anything.
- **Continuity says it is one world.** Pressed, the bars draw back and the name flies to the menu's corner instead of

- **Ask at the moment of commitment, and big enough to see.** Who you play comes up the first time a way in is taken,

**How the rain comes on (November 2026).** The owner found it abrupt: frame 7 began with the rain at once, the street
wet and nine in ten of the crowd's umbrellas up in one frame, each with no motion, and *It has begun to rain* said a
second and a half later over a street already soaked (the frame's `wet` snapped the weather on, and the crowd put its

her line, where this table had always put it. They asked whether a cut or a card should cover it: dark cloud gathering, and the
camera coming down on a crossing whose umbrellas are already up. Worked from first principles, a change of weather reads
as a switch when it has no warning, when everyone changes in the same instant, when each change has no motion, and when
it lands on no beat of the story; a cut hides all four instead of mending them, and this story pays for a cut more than
most: the frames run on in one evening with no cut between them, the only thing that bends time in it is the Stop (a
sky hurried past in a few seconds would be a second one), and the crossing blooming into umbrellas is the picture of the
place, not a seam to hide. Games that do this well in their engines (*Red Dead Redemption 2*'s storms, *Death
Stranding*'s timefall, *Ghost of Tsushima*'s wind as a cue, the walk-and-talks of *The Last of Us Part II*) give the
weather a warning, a first touch and a spread through the people, and let a big change happen while the player is
listening to someone, so that it is seen when they look up; games that set their weather by the chapter (*Yakuza*,
*Persona 5*'s calendar) cut between days, which this evening has not. Adopted, in the design only: the cloud lowers over
half a minute as the frame begins, while the player crosses to her (`rain_on: "meet"` on the frame, `story.js` `rain`;
the birds go and the leaves lift with it); her first word brings the first drops, and the rain builds over half a minute
while the street darkens as it wets; each of the crowd puts an umbrella up in their own moment, a few at the first drops
and most once the street is wet, the arm coming up and the canopy opening from furled about its tip in about a second
(`people.js` `umbOpening`, near and far alike), so that they go up behind her as she speaks, and when the talk is done
the crossing is umbrellas. The words come once the drops can be seen. No card and no cut: a card would tell what the
street can show. `tools/story_play.js` takes stills 0, 5, 10 and 20 seconds after her first word and fails rain before
it, or most umbrellas up within five seconds.

the viaduct. Rain, when it comes, is mixed under the voices as a soft, low wash, never a hiss, and
the Stop cuts it to silence. The evening opens quietly enough to hear one person say one line.

### The walk: the map with no story in it (November 2026)

The owner asked for a way to be in Shibuya without the case: after who you play, the story or a walk of your own. Worked
from what a player wants of it, not from the old free game this map once had:

- **What it is for.** To be at the crossing at an hour you choose and move at your own pace; to look round before the
  case, and to stay out in the night after it. The place is the whole of it: the hundred metres the hero level is, the
  crowd, the screens, the rain. Nothing in it is owed: no goal, no light standing in the street, no clock running out.
- **What it must not cost the story.** Nothing of the case is in it: no Stop, no afterimage, no 1964 in the finder, no
  dog. Walked first, it spoils nothing; walked after, it undoes nothing.
- **One world, not two games.** The same Kagami, the same evening's screens, the story's own card, type and amber. The
  map's old free game (`?walk=1`, *Tokyo Pilgrimage*, its quests, survey lens, height guess, passport, map that sends you
  anywhere, phone and taxi) stays a tool for building and checking; none of it is reachable from the story's page, in
  the story or on the walk (`game.js` `STORY_OFF`, the menu's old tour and passport put away), and the story's last card
  no longer opens it. Your car and the helicopter came back to the walk at the owner's word, in the walk's own interface
  (below, *Your car and the helicopter*).
- **One decision before you go.** The hour and the sky: the blue hour of the story's evening (10 October), the night
  after it, or the hour it is now in Tokyo; clear or rain. Whatever is chosen is the street behind the card at once, so
  the choice is made by looking, not by reading. The rest (any hour, any weather, the clock's speed) is in the menu.
- **What is not seen costs nothing.** It sets out where the story does, in the hero level, on foot, and offers nothing
  that sends you to the map's unfinished far corners; the map round you loads as you walk, as in the story.
- **Never stuck, never lost.** Where and when you are sits in the goal's corner (the area's name, the minute, the date
  and the sky, and under them what someone on the walk asked of you, with the metres to it); the keys once at the foot of
  the frame; at the top right your car, the camera (photo mode) and the menu, which has the hour, the sky and the
  story.
- **No page loaded again where one can be helped.** From the title the street on the card is the street walked. From
  the last card the walk is taken up where the case ended: the card fades off the street, the score lets go into the
  street's own sound over a few bars, the evening's people leave once the lens no longer has them (never in view), the
  dog is already on his way home, and the same night goes on round you, at its hour and under its sky. Only the way from
  the walk into the story opens the page again, straight into the first episode, as *Play it again* does.

What the games that have one do, and what was taken. *Assassin's Creed Origins*' Discovery Tour (2018) is a way in of
its own beside the campaign: the whole world to wander, with its quests, combat and time limits taken out. *Mafia:
Definitive Edition*'s Free Ride (2020) is chosen from the main menu, and in it the hour and the weather are the
player's, where the story's missions fix both. The *Yakuza* series' Premium Adventure (*Yakuza 0*, 2015, among others) is
the city after the story, free to roam with the story's world left as it was. Flight simulators ask the hour and the
weather before a free flight. And the long unnarrated walks through Tokyo filmed for the web, at night, in the rain, are
what a walk here is for. Adopted: Discovery Tour's separate way in, with the story's goals and its clock taken out;
Free Ride's hour and sky chosen before setting out and changed in the menu after; Premium Adventure's walk after the
story, from the place the story ends, without a lock (Yakuza opens it only after the end; this walk has nothing of the
case in it to keep back); the flight simulator's choice made over the live view. Nothing of those games' look or words is
used.

**The walk's opening (November 2026).** The owner asked for the walk, too, to open on the whole of the city from the air
and come down to the ground, as the story's opening does, but not the story's, and as a AAA game's would. Worked from
first principles:

- *What it is for.* The walk's promise is the place itself, at the hour you chose. So the opening goes from the whole to
  one kerb in it, the grammar of an establishing shot (the extreme wide, the wide, then the person), in one movement:
  the district to the horizon, the towers of the station, the crossing, your shoulder. Then the walk is yours.
- *Not the story's.* The story's opening circles the crossing from the south-west and cuts to the street, under its own
  title and the woman's words, at its own evening under a clear sky. The walk's comes in from the north-east, from 600 m
  over Omotesando with the evening's last light beyond the towers, in one flight with no cut, at the hour and under the
  sky the player chose (the rain falls in it; after dark it is a fifth brighter, as the walk's trailer's night aerials
  are). No guide, nothing of the case. Its title is the walk's own card in the title screen's type: WALK SHIBUYA, 渋谷,
  the date, hour and sky chosen over it in amber, the crossing's coordinates under it.
- *One flight.* The camera's way is one curve (`fly`: the points it passes, what it looks at from each, the lens at
  each) and the shots are only windows on it for the type and the score, so nothing jumps at a cut. It is flown at a
  pace set by its height over the ground: each metre counts as one over the height to the power 0.6, so 600 m up it
  drifts as a held reveal does, lower down the city streams past at a steady rate, and it slows into the hand-over. This
  is the fly-to of web maps and globes (J. J. van Wijk and W. A. A. Nuij, *Smooth and efficient zooming and panning*,
  2003, which Mapbox GL JS's and CesiumJS's flights follow: speed over the ground in proportion to what is seen),
  softened, since at the full rate the high part was a rush.
- *The beats.* `over` (8.5 s): from 600 m over Omotesando, the whole district to the horizon and the title. `glide`
  (8 s): down towards the station's towers. `edge` (5 s): at the deck's height to the north edge of SHIBUYA SKY's roof,
  its lit parapet ahead, and over it. `dive` (8.5 s): down the tower's face and out over the crossing to
  above where you stand, the crowd gathered at every kerb, and the walk turns green under you. `land` (6 s): straight
  down into the surge onto your shoulder, still looking across the crossing until the last second (`lookHold`), never
  tipped onto the tops of heads. Its way keeps clear of the roofs and of the trees' crowns: a first way down from the
  station's side passed through the twenty-metre zelkova on that corner.
- *A map without a map.* A few of the places the walk can take you are named as the camera passes over them
  (SHIBUYA SKY at 229 m, Hikarie, Cerulean Tower, Dogenzaka's 109, Center Gai, Hachiko): a point on
  the place, an amber stem, its name in its own script and in the title's type, faded in and out over its seconds and
  gone when it leaves the frame. Six, never more than three at once: what the walk offers, not a tour.
- *Its own sound.* A score of its own (film-score.js): E major on a glass bell over a piano's arpeggio, where the
  story's is a minor piano alone; rising, never striking; the crossing's birds as it turns green; let go into the
  street's own sound as you land. The loading cue goes as the walk sets out.
- *Never in the way.* Any key or a tap eases down to your shoulder in under a second. With reduced motion or
  `?overture=0` the crane comes down from the title's view as before; taken up from the story's last card, on a fresh
  page too, the walk goes on at the kerb without it, as it does in place. The walk's own people are out in it already. The
  camera does not come down to the kerb before the level you stand in is drawn: the film holds at the top of its last
  shot while it is not, six seconds at most. Its score is rendered and its parts compiled while the hour and the sky are
  chosen, so Set out plays it at once.

What was taken, and from where (design only; nothing of their look, words or code): the opening that comes down from a
whole city to a few people in it (*West Side Story*, 1961, which opens looking straight down on Manhattan and comes
down into one playground; *Grand Theft Auto V*, 2013, whose change of character drops from the map's height to one
person in the street); the unbroken flight of the FPV drone one-takes (*Right Up Our Alley*, 2021, one flight through a
whole building); the fly-to of web maps and globes for its pace (above; the idea, from the paper, not code); and the
viewpoints of the *Assassin's Creed* games, from which the city round you is shown and its places put on your map, for
the names. On the page: `cities/shibuya/city.json` `game.walk.overture`, `web/game/overture.js` (`fly`, `marks`, guide
`none`, the title's `style: card`, `{tokens}` the game fills, the hold for the level), `web/game/game.js` (`startWalk`,
`walkFilmMake`). `tools/walk_open.js` takes a still of each shot and checks the camera's clearance over the roofs and
from the trees' crowns (`FILM=1`: the whole film with its score); `tools/walk_play.js` sees it play and a key or a tap end it.

**The title's ways in, one list (November 2026).** On a phone the walk's button stood taller than the episode's (its
second line wrapped under it), and on a desktop the title's ways in were three widths and three looks: an amber one, a
paper one, a grey one. The owner asked how a AAA game sets this, and for the walk first while the episodes are being
finished. A main menu in a game of that kind (*The Last of Us Part II*, *Ghost of Tsushima*, among others) is one list
over the live scene: every entry in one type and size, one under another, one entry selected from the start and the
selection the only thing lit, moved by the arrows or the d-pad and pressed by Enter or A. Adopted (`story.css` "the
title's ways in", `walk.js`, `game.js` `titleLead`): the ways in are one list, every row the same width and height and
each in two lines, its name and a word on it (*Walk Shibuya · No story, any hour, any sky*; *Episode 1 · You came late ·
The story, 7 frames, one evening*). The walk is the first row and the one Enter and a pad's A take, with the amber
hairline and the Enter key on it; the story and the options are under it, and while a case is going *Continue* stands over
it (since: *The menu, by what this browser has done*, under *The title, as a game opens*). The
hand put on another row (the mouse, Tab, up and down, the d-pad, which moves on from the first row rather than from the
portraits) fills that row with the amber and takes the key with it. On a phone the rows run the card's width, 64 px
high, over the 44 points and 48 dp a touch target asks (Apple's and Google's guidelines); on a phone on its side they
stand side by side, still one size, so that every way in is on the screen. What the list opens is set the same way:
the walk's step (its hours and skies even tiles, the name over the hour, Set out and Back two halves of one row) and a
closed case's frames (two even columns), each as wide as the list. `tools/walk_play.js` and `tools/title_menu.js` fail
a list not led by the walk or rows not all one size. To put the episode first again, `walk.js` appends its button instead
of prepending it and leaves the episode its `primary`.

**Your car and the helicopter (November 2026).** The owner asked for the walk to drive and fly as well. Worked from first
principles rather than from the old free game's panels:

- *Each way of getting about is a scale of looking.* On foot the hundred metres of the hero level take minutes and every
  kerb is seen; a car crosses them in seconds and shows the streets as a sequence; the helicopter shows the district as
  a map. A free roam is richer for all three, as long as the map round you can be built as fast as you cross it: a car's
  50 km/h is well within what the map loads round a walker, and from the air the far streets are the simplified middle
  distance they are built as (docs/PERFORMANCE.md). On a phone both keep to its district, as walking does.
- *The way in is a thing in the street, not a menu.* Your car waits at a kerb by the crossing (`game.start.car`); the
  helicopter stands on the pad on SHIBUYA SKY's roof, up the SKY GATE lift, which makes the lift a way somewhere as well as
  a view. E (or a tap on the prompt) gets in beside either, and out once it has stopped or landed. The car can be called to
  the kerb nearest you from the menu (*Bring your car*), as a free ride's own car or a whistled horse comes; the
  helicopter is not called (it has a place to stand, and finding it is part of having it), but left anywhere and walked
  far from, it is back on the nearest pad when you come by one again (`heliToPad`).
- *Nothing to fail.* No police, no damage, no fuel, no timer: a bump is said and forgotten, R puts the car back on the
  road, and a person in the road stops the car rather than the car stopping them.
- *One interface.* The keys for each are shown once at the foot of the frame, the first time you are in it on a walk, in
  the walk's own line (not the old game's coach and dashboard); the corner under the hour gives the one number each
  needs, the speed, and in the air the height over the ground to set it down by (and the rotor while it spools up). On a
  phone the stick drives or flies, and beside it are the hand brake, the horn and the view in the car, up, down and the
  view in the air.
- *The story keeps none of it.* In an episode nothing but the story's own rides are offered (`currentAction`: an action
  marked `walk` passes on the walk only).

What the games that do this do, and what was taken (design only; nothing of their look, words or code): *Grand Theft

**Your car in plain sight (November 2026).** The owner found the car's call too well hidden: it was a line in the menu,
and a player who did not open the menu never learnt the car would come. Now it is the first of the walk's buttons at the
top right (*Your car*, with C on a keyboard; on a phone its icon over the camera's), shown whenever you are on foot, and
C is in the keys' line as you set out. Called, it does not appear at the kerb: it comes up the kerb's lane over the last
twenty metres, in at 30 km/h and slowing evenly, and stops beside you with a toot (`game.js` `bringCar`, `carComing`),
as the cars that come when called in *Cyberpunk 2077* and *Mafia* arrive rather than appear. Where there is no room to
come from (traffic there, a wall), it stands at the kerb at once, as before. Mr Oda's story (below) sends you to it.

**People with something to say (November 2026).** The owner asked for the walk to have people to talk to, with stories,
and a speech mark over those who can talk, as the big open-world games do. Worked from first principles:

- *A street with nobody to talk to is a set.* What makes a crossing a place is its people, and the walk had only the
  crowd going by. So a few people of this evening stand where they would: Mei, waiting at Hachiko for a friend who is
  late; Riko, the friend, waiting at the station's Hachiko exit across the crossing; Mr Oda, a retired taxi driver who
  still comes down at night with a can of hot coffee; Shun at his shop in Center Gai; Yuna at SKY GATE's lift; Taro, a
  photographer on the roof by SKY EDGE; and Aoi at the arch, with a word in passing.
- *Small stories in parts, each asking one thing the walk can do.* Mei's (*Which Hachiko*) crosses the scramble on the
  green, twice; Mr Oda's (*Once round by car*) drives your car to 109; Shun's (*The shot everyone takes*) is a photograph
  of the crossing from in it or from high up; Yuna's (*Forty-five floors*) is the lift to the roof; and Taro's (*Above
  the crossing*, added at the owner's asking) is the helicopter on the roof's pad, flown out over the crossing and the
  crossing photographed from the air (the camera holds the world still while you frame it), then set down on the roof
  again to show him: it takes only a photograph taken from the helicopter, over the crossing and sixty metres up or
  more, never one from the roof on foot. So the side stories are how the walk's ways about are found, as an open
  world's first side quests teach its systems.
- *Nothing of the case in any of them.* Walked before the story they spoil nothing; walked after, they undo nothing.
  Their theme is the evening's own, lightly: two friends waiting on two sides of one name, which is what the story is
  about, without a word of it.
- *Found, never owed.* A speech mark over the head of whoever has something to say: amber, its dots breathing, while they
  have a part of a story for you (seen from 24 metres), pale for a word in passing (from eighteen); their name and what
  they are under it once you are within eleven metres. A mark is only ever over someone the player can see: their head
  in sight of the lens (a ray against the city's solids, `ctx.seen`, asked every eighth frame), and it fades in and out
  over a quarter of a second as they step behind something. It was seen from 42 m and through anything: on SHIBUYA
  SKY's roof Taro's mark stood over the glass with him out of sight behind the heliport's deck, a few pixels of a man on a
  phone if he was seen at all (the owner's report, November 2026). E (or a tap on the prompt) beside them opens the talk in
  the story's own box, with the story's conversation camera cutting between you. What they asked sits under the hour in
  the corner, with the metres to it and the key that does it; the line turns amber when it is done, and the corner then
  sends you back to them. Where they are on another level, as Yuna is at the lift's foot when you have done what she asked
  on the roof 226 m over her, the corner names the way (*Down the lift to Yuna*) and its metres are to the lift's stop on
  your level (`ctx.via`, from `game.json` `lifts`); it read *Back to Yuna · 15 m*, the metres in plan to a point under
  your feet. Someone sent on ahead (Riko) is there when you come back, never seen going. The last part said,
  the story's name comes up over the street with how many of the walk's stories you have heard (*A story of the walk ·
  1 of 5*). Where each has got to is kept in this browser; no story has a clock, a fail or a reward. The walk's menu lists
  them as a game's journal lists its side stories: each by its name, ticked when told, with what was asked while it is
  under way, and for one not yet met a word on where to look (*By Hachiko, someone is looking for a friend who is
  late*), never a pin on a map.
- *The street talks too.* The overheard lines of the crossing (`game.json` `chatter`: *Meet at Hachiko? Everyone says
  that.*) are drawn on the walk, in the walk's glass and type rather than the old game's white bubbles. Each line is
  said by someone standing there: of the crowd drawn within five metres of where it is said, in the lens's frame and in
  sight, the nearest (the next nearest for the other side of a conversation), and the bubble follows them as they walk
  (`game.js` `speakers`, `places.js`). With nobody there in sight, nobody says it, and a line said off the side of the
  screen is not shown: on the roof's empty deck at night a visitor's line hung over the glass, held at the screen's edge,

**Somewhere to sit (November 2026).** There was no bench within 150 m of the crossing, and sitting existed only for a

- *A calm camera that frames the show.* Seated, the camera stands on a tripod a few metres behind you and to your left, at
  eye height and tilted a few degrees down: you sit at the side of the frame, the crowd fills the middle and the screens

### The play rebuilt from first principles (18 November 2026)

The owner asked whether the play could be raised without adding a system: the story has one verb, the camera, so the
way up is for each act of it, and each answer, to carry more of the player's own judgement. The proposal put was to
answer by presenting evidence against testimony, as *Ace Attorney* does, instead of choosing one of three sentences.
It was tested against what a deduction owes its player. (The other, the last frame taken as the two meet, is the
section above.)

What a deduction owes the player, in four lines: the answer is made by the player, not picked out of a short list the
author wrote; the space of answers is too large to guess in, or guessing costs something; the game confirms reasoning
and a wrong answer points at what to look at again; and nothing on the screen gives the answer away. Read against them:

| Where | What it asked | What failed |
|---|---|---|
| 5, Tatsuo | *He says he was in the cut all afternoon, minding the pumps. Tell him what the roof saw*, then a print | The question named both the lie and the evidence. The story's one liar, and the one place *Ace Attorney* was promised, asked nothing |
| 7, Sumiko | a print, then one of three sentences, for each of three questions | The question named the print (*show me where it changed hands*), the print gave the sentence (the foreman's own frame, then *the foreman, her brother*), and the right sentence was the long, specific one. Two of the three were things a witness had said outright: a test of memory dressed as a deduction |

**Evidence against testimony fits a liar, and the story has one.** Contradiction is the strongest deduction a game
has: two things the player holds that cannot both be true, an answer space that is the product of statements and
prints, and no answer text to give it away. But it needs a claim to break. Tatsuo makes four, in his own words (the
river was put under the street in time; the gang was off for the ceremony; he was in the cut all afternoon minding the
pumps; nobody from his gang went up on any roof). Three are true or are a dodge the roll cannot touch; one is broken by
what Kanbara saw from the parapet at three, an empty cut. So frame 5 lays out his four statements; the player picks the
one the roll will not let stand, then the print that breaks it, and both are judged together (`press`, `lie`, `frame`;
`story.js` `cross`). A true statement pressed is answered as a true one (*Every man of them. The section chief sent
them home himself*), which is itself a clue; the lie with the wrong print is shrugged off. Two wrong and he knows a
guess, as before. Sixteen pairs and two tries: a guesser fails nearly always, and a player who heard Kanbara does not.

**Sumiko is not lying, so she is not cross-examined.** Her questions are the challenge to the reader: who, why and
who. Pressing her lines would have made the climax a trial of a woman who has waited sixty-two years. What the
challenge needs is that the player name the answer. So each is answered by naming one of the case's seven people (the
episode's `people`: what the player knows each of them as, and the drowned boy only if Tatsuo was made to say his name),
and then showing the print or the pair that proves it (`who`, `answer`, `proof`; `story.js` `summation`). Name first, so
the print cannot give the name away; and the question no longer says which print. A wrong name is answered for that
name (`wrong_by`): *I tore free and ran, and left him on his feet*, said to a player who named her for the push. The
second question is the title's: *Why did Akira not cross the street? Tell me whom he was keeping out of it.* Its answer
is the one inference in the case that nobody says aloud, and the player says it to her face: Hayase Sumiko. The third
asks who the boy was (Mr Tanaka, proved by his pitch), and then she asks whether there was nothing to say: the player
gives her the sentence the boy never said, sixty-two years late. That is the evening's verb in one line: carrying across
what he could not. Names are short enough to need no new interface on a phone (seven in two columns), and a pad moves
through them as it moves through any answers.

Not taken, and why: answering Sumiko with a line chosen from the log (*present a testimony*). It turns the challenge
into finding a sentence in a hundred, and it loses the one inference: nobody says that Akira believed she had done it,
so no line can be presented for it.

| *Return of the Obra Dinn* | is never confirmed alone: the book says *correct* only when three fates hold together, so a guess cannot be checked one at a time | the logbook, the crew list and every memory are at hand |
| *L.A. Noire* | an accusation of a lie needs the evidence from the notebook; a wrong one loses the line of questioning, not the case | the notebook, always |

Adopted: the case is the player's to argue, so nothing of it is played twice for one answer. A wrong answer is answered
for what was said, as before (*I tore free and ran, and left him on his feet*), and costs something the player can see:
it is written into the file in the fountain pen's ink and struck through as it is written (the slip at the top of the
frame says *Entered, and struck through*), and the last card counts it (`story.js` `judged`, the question's `file`).
The second wrong answer on one question says more (`wrong2`: where to look, in her words) and marks the print that
answers it when the roll is laid out (*look again*), as a judge would point at the record. The third, and she says it
herself (`told`): the case goes on, and the file and the last card keep that she had to (*She answered one question
herself*). A right answer is written down too, and when all three are settled the file says in one line that they hold
together, and how many were the player's own (Obra Dinn's confirmation, given once the whole case stands). The roll and
the file are at hand from inside every question: one more answer, *The roll and the file*, opens them over it, and
closing them asks the same question again, the name already given kept if it was the print being asked for
(`atHand`). Kept: the name first and then the print, judged as a pair, so that a guesser of seven names and as many
prints is wrong, and seen to be wrong, nearly every time. Not taken: a penalty bar. Sumiko is not a court; she is a woman
who has waited sixty-two years, and her patience is the story's subject, not a meter.

**The first Stop shows her (frame 1, the walk to Sumiko).** The players spent about four minutes each between the world
stopping and reaching her. In the stills of a first play the reason is plain: the view after the dog's entrance looks
north across the crossing, and she is thirty metres off at the far kerb behind a hundred people standing exactly as
still as she does; the one thing that told her from them was that she turns her head. The goal said *Walk to her*, and
the dog led, but a player has to see where before they follow. *Journey* puts a light on the mountain and never says a
word about it; level designers lead the eye with a clear line through a crowded frame; *The Last Guardian* and *Ghost of
Tsushima* show the way with something of the world (the creature's gaze, the wind) instead of a marker. Adopted, with no
word added: under the flash of the Stop, where nobody is seen to move, the held crowd stands out of a lane from you to
her a little wider than two people walking abreast (`story.js` `stop`, `ctx.clear`), so that she stands at the end of an
open line through the crossing and the dog runs down it; and she has the halation an old exposure has round its
brightest part, a soft pale column about her that is found across the street and fades as you come near, so that close
to she is only a person (`game.js` `storyFigure` `glow`, on while she is looked for). Every afterimage that is walked to
has the same.

**The question the detective waits on (frame 3, after its print).** One player sat half an hour in this beat; the page
recorded the roll being opened in it. After the print the detective asks the player to say it was her, or not: a choice
with no right answer and no time, the first time the story asks the player to commit, and it sends them to the roll to
weigh it, with the question waiting under the roll and nothing on the screen to say so. The *Telltale* adventures treat
silence as an answer: the choice has a visible time, and saying nothing is remembered like anything said. Adopted: he
waits thirty seconds (`wait_s`), shown as a hairline under the answers running down, held while the roll, the log or the
menu is open; when it has run out the player has said nothing, which he answers as *not yet* (`silent`: *You say
nothing. Good.*), and the file writes *You said nothing at the police box when he waited for her name*. The roll opened
over a waiting question closes on *Back to the question*.

**The office's call (frame 1, after the first Stop).** Five long lines, about seven minutes a player, one of them for
twenty-five minutes before reloading the page: a call is a thing heard, and it waited on a key at every line.
*Firewatch*'s radio talk runs on by itself at the pace it is spoken, and is still the game's whole story. Adopted: three
lines with the same facts (who Kagami is to the office, the kerb, the client and his hour, the word on the plan, the
sister), and every call (the prologue, a frame's message, the call before the helicopter) goes on by itself once a line
is whole, after the time it takes to read it (`hud.js` `dialogue`, `auto`); a key or a tap still goes on at once, and
it waits while the menu, the log or the roll is up. A box that waits on an answer (a question) waits; a box that only
speaks never does.

`tools/story_play.js` plays it: `SLIP=7` answers the first question wrongly three times and must see her say it herself
and the last card count it, never the card that takes the frame up again; the first question of every play opens the
roll and the file from inside it and must be asked again; `SILENT=3` leaves the detective's question to run out; and
`AFTER=1` with `UPTO=n` plays on past frame n's print, through what is said after it, so that a frame's call is played
with it.

## 13. What is left, and the marks without colour

What issue 10 asked that a container cannot finish is planned and split off: recorded Japanese voices and faces that
hold a close shot ([VOICES.md](VOICES.md); issues 11 and 12), the hundred metres on a real GPU against photographs
(issue 13), and playtests with people who have not seen the story ([PLAYTEST.md](PLAYTEST.md); issue 14).

The marks do not depend on colour: their kinds are told apart by shape and named in words, and an afterimage's speech
mark is drawn in a broken line as well as in blue, so it is told from the living's without its colour. An answer the
hand is on (the arrows on a keyboard, the d-pad on a pad) has an outline as well as its amber.

What is left of the story's own, each with its issue: the living Sumiko's face in the summation's close shot does not read as a Japanese woman of 84 (issue 34); the old
woman's clear umbrella is not built. Frame 5's place, plain boxes and haze round its print until November 2026 (issue
33), is a hero level of its own: the Ginza line station's underside, piers and the east exit's ground (docs/HERO.md,
"Miyamasu").
